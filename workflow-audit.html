What an audit produces.

We take one process your team repeats, such as vendor bills, onboarding paperwork, intake or a weekly report, and follow it the way it really runs. That includes the steps that live in someone's head, the spreadsheet nobody mentions and the exceptions that break the documented version. We sit with the person who does the work, look at a handful of real examples and mark where the time goes. Read the method.

Audits end in one of three places, and all are good outcomes. Sometimes the team needs skill, not software, and that becomes an enablement workshop on their own examples. Sometimes the repeated part should be a system, and that becomes a workflow automation or custom agent; in Tampa Bay, see our workflow automation consultant in Tampa page. And sometimes the process is not ready, because the inputs are inconsistent or nobody owns it, and we say so. Nothing else has to follow the audit.

What you receive.

  • The real path

    How the work actually moves, including the exceptions.

  • Where the drag is

    Rework, waiting, duplicate entry and handoffs that stall.

  • The control path

    Permissions, limits, fallback behavior, logs and ownership, defined before any system goes live.

  • The recommendation

    Train, automate, or leave it alone, with the reasoning attached.

Questions teams ask before an audit.

What is a workflow audit?

A workflow audit is a structured pass over one repeated business process that documents its real inputs, decisions, tools, handoffs, review points, and failure modes. The output is a decision: enable the team to do the work better with AI, automate the repeated parts, or leave the process alone.

Why not skip discovery and start building?

Because automating a process nobody can explain is the quickest way to a system nobody trusts. If the team cannot say where judgment belongs and what good output looks like, an automation will only produce wrong answers faster. The audit is the place to find that out before you pay for a build.

What makes a workflow a good candidate?

Repeated work with clear inputs, useful outputs, a natural review point, and measurable drag. Processes that run weekly or daily, have an obvious owner, and produce something a person currently checks by hand are the strongest candidates.

What should we prepare?

Three or four real examples of the work: what came in, what went out, and anything that went wrong. Bring the person who does the work, not only the person who manages it, and a list of the tools it touches. Screenshots are fine.
