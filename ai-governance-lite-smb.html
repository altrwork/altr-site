AI governance for small business: a policy that fits on two pages.

A small business does not need a compliance department to govern AI. It needs to know where AI is already used, what data may go into it, who approves what, and what happens when something goes wrong. In 2026 it also needs a rule for the personal AI agents employees are connecting to their work email.

Short answer

AI governance for a small business is a short written policy plus a list of where AI is used. It names approved tools, sorts data into fine, needs approval and never, sets who approves new uses, requires human review of anything customer-facing, bans connecting personal AI agents to work accounts without approval, and says who to tell when something goes wrong.

What does AI governance mean for a small business?

It means operating rules, not a framework document. For a team of five to a hundred people, governance is working when anyone can answer four questions without asking: which tools are approved, what may I put in them, who checks the output, and who do I tell if something goes wrong.

Everything below produces one of seven things: an inventory of AI use, data tiers, approval tiers, an acceptable-use rule for personal agents, a vendor check, a review rule, and an incident path. Most small teams can draft all seven in an afternoon and refine them over the following month.

How do you find out where AI is already used?

Ask, without blame, and write it down. Employees are almost always using AI tools already, some on personal accounts. An amnesty-style survey gets a truer picture than a ban, and the inventory it produces is the base for every other rule.

What data can employees put into AI tools?

Sort data into three tiers and name the categories in each, so nobody has to interpret an abstract word like "confidential".

  • Fine to use in approved tools: public information, your own marketing copy, internal notes with no personal or client detail, general research.
  • Needs approval, and only in a company-controlled workspace: customer records, contracts, pricing, financial reports, employee information, anything under a client confidentiality clause.
  • Never: passwords and API keys, full payment card or bank details, and regulated data (health, government ID numbers, anything a law or contract restricts) unless a specific tool has been approved for it.

The tier depends on the account as much as the data. Business plans from the major vendors state that they do not train on your content by default; consumer accounts have different defaults and sit outside your admin controls. Company work belongs in company accounts.

What should the policy say about personal AI agents?

Personal AI agents are a new category, and the one most small-business policies do not cover yet. Unlike a chat window, an agent asks for standing access to your accounts and acts on its own. The rule an SMB needs is simple: no personal agent is connected to a work email, calendar, drive, messaging or payment account without written approval.

Two examples show why this is a 2026 problem. Meta launched Muse on September 8, 2026, as a consumer personal agent in the US on iOS, Android and the web. Meta pitches it as an agent that runs errands and manages schedules across apps such as email, calendars and payments. The user chooses whether it may only read their mail or also send on their behalf, and it can open a browser and fill in forms. Instinct, an invite-only assistant people reach by text or phone, raised a $250 million Series B in August 2026 and links to email, calendars and messaging apps to act on a user's behalf.

Neither is a business product, and that is the point. When an employee authorizes a consumer agent on a work mailbox, client correspondence flows to a vendor under that person's consumer terms, not under any agreement your company signed, and the agent can send mail or make commitments in the employee's name. The risk is not that these products are malicious. It is that nobody at the company chose them. Our guide to personal AI agents for business covers the products in more detail.

A clause you can adapt:

Personal AI agents. Do not connect any AI assistant or agent to a
company email, calendar, file storage, messaging, CRM or payment
account unless it appears on the approved tools list. This includes
consumer agents that ask to read or send email, manage your calendar,
use a browser for you or make purchases. Personal use on personal
accounts is your business; company accounts and company data are not.
If an agent would help your work, ask [owner] and we will review it.

Back the rule with the setting that enforces it. In Google Workspace, an admin can restrict which third-party apps may access Gmail, Drive and Calendar under Security, API controls, App access control, and block apps that have not been reviewed. In Microsoft 365, Entra ID's user consent settings can require admin approval before any app is granted access to company data. Either one turns the policy from a request into a control.

Who approves a new AI use?

Use three tiers so that most uses need no meeting at all.

  • Low risk, manager awareness: drafting internal documents, summarizing public material, brainstorming, in an approved tool.
  • Medium risk, owner sign-off: anything using tier-two data, any new connector to a company system, any output that reaches a customer after human review.
  • High risk, leadership approval: anything that makes or recommends decisions about employment, credit, pricing for an individual, legal positions or financial commitments, and any agent that can act without a person approving each step.

How should a small business review an AI vendor?

Before a tool becomes part of daily work, answer these on one page and file it:

  1. Does the vendor train on our content, and what is the default on the plan we would buy?
  2. How long is data retained, and can we delete it?
  3. Is there single sign-on, and can we remove a departing employee's access in one place?
  4. What can the tool reach on its own (mailbox, drive, calendar, browser), and can an admin limit it?
  5. Is there an admin log of who used what?
  6. Can we export our work if we leave?
  7. What happens to the workflow if the tool is down or the price changes?

The outcome is one of three: approved, approved with limits (name the limits), or rejected. Record the owner and the renewal date.

What review, logging and incident rules are enough?

A person checks anything that goes to a customer, a regulator or an employee file, and anything involving money or legal terms, before it leaves. Internal drafts can be spot-checked.

Logging can be light: keep the inventory current and note issues against the row they belong to. For incidents, write down five names: who receives the report, who pauses the workflow or revokes the tool's access, who checks what was affected, who tells anyone impacted, and who approves a restart. Revoking access matters more now that agents hold standing permissions; know where in your admin console that is done before you need it.

Working rule

Every AI use has an owner, an approved account, a data tier and a reviewer. No agent touches a company account unless someone chose it on purpose.

Common questions

Does a small business need an AI policy?

Yes, if anyone on the team uses AI tools, which is nearly every team. A one or two page policy covering approved tools, data tiers, review rules, personal agents and who to tell when something goes wrong prevents most problems and takes an afternoon to draft.

What should an AI acceptable use policy include?

Approved tools and accounts, what data may go in and what never may, who approves new uses, which outputs need human review, a rule on connecting AI agents to company accounts, and the person to contact when something goes wrong. Keep it short enough that people read it.

Can employees connect personal AI agents like Meta Muse to work email?

They should not without approval. Consumer agents such as Muse ask for access to email, calendars and payments and can act on the user's behalf, under consumer terms your company never agreed to. Require written approval, and use your Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 admin settings to block unreviewed apps.

Should we ban AI tools until the policy is written?

Usually not. A ban pushes use onto personal accounts where you cannot see it. Approve one business-plan tool quickly, tell people what data stays out, and write the fuller policy over the following weeks.

Who should own AI governance in a small company?

One named person, often the operations lead or a founder, who keeps the tool list current, answers questions and approves new uses. It does not need to be a full-time role, but it needs to be someone specific.
