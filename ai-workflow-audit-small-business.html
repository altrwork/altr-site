How to run an AI workflow audit yourself, before automating anything.

You do not need a consultant to find out whether a process is ready for AI. You need one real example of the work, the person who does it, and an hour with the worksheet below. What comes out is a decision about each step: leave it, help the person do it faster, or automate it.

Short answer

To run an AI workflow audit before automating, pick one process that repeats weekly, walk through a real recent instance with the person who does it, and record each step's input, action, output, reviewer and what goes wrong. Mark every step as a fixed rule, a judgment call or a decision. Time the current process, then automate only the rule steps and assist the judgment ones.

Which workflow should you audit first?

Pick one that repeats at least weekly, has a single owner, and produces something a person can check. The best first candidate is usually the task people complain about, not the one that sounds most impressive.

  • Repeats often. Weekly or daily. A quarterly task is rarely worth building around.
  • One owner. Someone who does it and can say whether a change helped.
  • Checkable output. An email, a report, a record in a system, a quote. Not "better decisions".
  • Written inputs. Emails, forms, PDFs, spreadsheets. Work that lives in phone calls and memory is harder to change.

Skip processes that are being reorganized, that depend on one system you are about to replace, or where nobody agrees how it should work. Fix those first.

How do you map the workflow as it actually runs?

Sit with the person who does the work and have them do a real, recent instance while you write down every step. Do not work from the documented process; it is usually wrong in the places that matter.

  1. Choose a specific instance from the last week, such as one actual quote request.
  2. Start at the moment the work arrives and end when the output leaves their hands.
  3. Write each step as a verb and an object: "copy address into the CRM", "check price sheet", "ask Sam about the discount".
  4. Note every system, file and person touched, including the spreadsheet nobody mentioned.
  5. Ask where it went wrong the last three times. The exceptions are where most of the time goes.

How do you decide which steps to automate?

Apply the same test to every row.

  • Automate when the input is structured, the rule can be written down completely, and a mistake would be caught or is cheap. A tool like Zapier or Make handles these; no model is needed.
  • Assist when the step needs reading or writing and a person reviews the result anyway: drafting, summarizing, extracting fields from a varied document. This is where a prompt, a shared Project or an AI step fits.
  • Leave with a person when the step is a decision with money, legal, employment or relationship consequences, or when nobody can say what a good answer looks like.

If most rows are "Assist", the answer is usually training and a few good prompts, not software. Zapier vs AI agents goes deeper on where the line between rules and judgment falls.

What should you measure before changing anything?

Record a baseline now, or you will not be able to tell later whether anything improved. Rough numbers from the person doing the work are enough.

  • How many times the workflow runs in a normal week.
  • Minutes per instance, timed on three real instances, not estimated.
  • How often the output is sent back or corrected, and why.
  • How long work waits between steps, which is often more than the work itself.

When is a workflow not ready for AI?

Stop and fix the process first if any of these are true. Automating on top of them produces wrong answers faster.

  • Two people doing the same task describe different steps.
  • Inputs arrive in several places under different names, or are often incomplete.
  • No one can say what a correct output looks like.
  • There is no point where a person reviews the result before it matters.
  • The volume is so low that maintaining an automation would cost more time than it saves.

When should you bring someone in?

Run it yourself first; most small teams can. Outside help earns its cost when the workflow crosses several teams and no one person sees all of it, when the systems involved need a custom connection, or when you want the audit and the team training done together. Working rule

Automate a step only after you can name its input, rule, output, reviewer and failure mode, and you have timed how long it takes today.

Common questions

What is an AI workflow audit?

A step-by-step map of one repeated process that records each step's input, action, output, reviewer and failure mode, then marks which steps to automate, which to assist with AI and which to leave with a person. It is done before anything is built.

How long does it take to audit one workflow yourself?

For a single process with one owner, the mapping usually takes an hour or two with the person who does the work, plus a week of timing a few real instances. Processes that cross several teams take longer because every handoff needs both sides in the room.

Do I need special software to run a workflow audit?

No. A spreadsheet with one row per step and columns for input, rule or judgment, output, reviewer, failure mode and decision is enough. What matters is walking through a real recent instance with the person who does the work.

What should I automate first after the audit?

The steps with structured inputs, a rule you can write down completely and a cheap or easily caught failure, such as creating records or moving files. Leave judgment steps to AI-assisted drafts that a person reviews.

Can AI help run the audit itself?

Yes, for the paperwork. Claude or ChatGPT can turn a dictated walkthrough into a step list, or group a week of calendar entries by task type. The judgment about which steps matter and what can go wrong still has to come from the people doing the work.
