How we work

We bill clients by saying who to bill.

A working session ends. Somebody has to bill for it, and that somebody is one of two people who would both rather be on the next call. So instead of opening a dashboard we just say it — invoice them a thousand dollars for the enablement session — and the invoice exists a second later, drafted and priced, with a link to read.

That is the whole interaction. Typed or spoken, one sentence. It is not that the old way was hard; it was fiddly enough to postpone. Find the client, start an invoice, type the amount, pick the terms, check every field, wonder whether this firm is already in there from last quarter. Six small steps, none of them difficult, all of them easy to push to Friday.

The good part is what gets decided underneath without anybody choosing it. It looks the client up by email and reuses the record, so a firm we bill monthly builds one history instead of twelve near-duplicates. It puts ACH ahead of card, so the payment page opens on bank transfer — $5 in fees on a $1,000 invoice instead of $29.30. Nobody toggles that. It is just how our invoices go out, and across a year it is real money we never had to think about.

Then it stops, on purpose. The script cannot finalise an invoice and cannot email one. Drafting is the tedious part and should be instant; sending is where a wrong number reaches a client, so the last click is human. A separate job runs overnight and checks our records against Stripe, in case a payment notification went missing and somebody is sitting in the wrong state. It is boring and it earns its keep about twice a year.

None of this is clever engineering — it is a couple of hundred lines against an API that was already good. What it buys a two-person company is that billing stops being a task anybody schedules, dreads or forgets. It happens in the thirty seconds after the work finishes, while the details are still fresh, which is also exactly when a client is most ready to pay.
